Your Ely farm: where the emissions come from, and what three options would do

Prepared for: A tenant farmer near Ely (180 ha: potatoes and wheat on deep peat, grass, suckler cows and ewes) (persona: Farmer (owner-occupier or tenant) or land manager) · 18 November 2026 · SLATE digital-twin orchestrator

Question. I'm a tenant farmer near Ely on 180 ha: 60 ha of potatoes and 40 ha of winter wheat on deep peat, 80 ha of grass, 90 suckler cows with followers and 200 breeding ewes, and a few kilometres of hedges. Where do most of my farm's emissions come from, and what would raising the water table on the potato ground, a methane-reducing feed additive and planting hedgerow trees each do?

As understood. Where do most of the greenhouse-gas emissions on a 180 ha tenanted farm near Ely come from? The farm has 60 ha of potatoes and 40 ha of winter wheat on deep peat, 80 ha of grass, 90 suckler cows with followers, 200 breeding ewes and a few kilometres of hedges. And how much would each of these change emissions: (1) raising the water table on the potato ground, (2) a methane-reducing feed additive, (3) planting hedgerow trees?

Key findings

  • The drained peat is by far the biggest source. The 100 ha of potatoes and wheat give off about 3,710 t CO2e a year: about 37 t CO2e per hectare, mostly CO2 from the peat breaking down [R1, R2]. That is roughly 88% of the farm total we could model.
  • Methane from the livestock comes to about 504 t CO2e a year (18.7 t of methane). About 87% of that comes from the cattle and 13% from the sheep. Nearly all of it comes from digestion (enteric methane) rather than manure [R6].
  • Raising the water table on the 60 ha of potatoes is the biggest lever. If you keep growing potatoes, raising the yearly average water table from about 0.66 m to 0.45 m below the surface cuts about 770 t CO2e a year, and raising it to 0.30 m cuts about 1,410 t. Rewetting the land to grow reed at about 0.20 m cuts about 2,100 t a year [R1, R3–R5].
  • A feed additive fed to the cattle cuts about 59 t CO2e a year if it works out at a 15% herd-average reduction, or about 118 t at 30%. That is about 1.5–3% of the farm total [R6–R8].
  • 100 new hedgerow trees would take up on average about 0.4–1.0 t CO2 a year over 50 years, or 18–48 t in total over that time [R9]. That is small for climate, but trees can do other jobs on the farm.

What this means

What drives your footprint. Almost nine-tenths of your modelled emissions come from the peat itself, not from what you grow or graze on it. Draining keeps the water table about two-thirds of a metre down, so air gets into the peat and it slowly breaks down, giving off CO2 and some nitrous oxide. Each hectare of drained potato or wheat ground gives off about as much as the whole cattle herd does per 12 ha of peat. Put another way, 100 ha of peat gives off more than seven times the herd's methane. Wheat and potatoes come out about the same per hectare (about 37 t CO2e/ha). Potatoes give off more N2O and wheat a little more CO2 [R1, R2].

How the options compare. Raising the water table is the only one of the three options at the scale of your footprint. Even a partial rise under potatoes, to 0.45 m, saves about 13 times as much as the more optimistic feed-additive case. Raising it to 0.30 m roughly halves the potato ground's emissions per hectare. Rewetting to a wetland crop such as reed would cut those 60 ha by about 95% per hectare in the model. That would end potato production on that ground, so it is a change of land use, not a tweak. The feed additive is a real but modest cut, at most about 3% of the total. How well it works depends on how many days the cattle are housed and fed a ration. Hedgerow trees store carbon slowly. Their climate value on this farm is small, but they bring shade, shelter and wildlife benefits that this model does not count.

What the model knows about your farm and what it assumes. It does not know your actual water levels, fertiliser use or follower numbers. It uses typical Fenland values and a herd we assumed for you (see caveats). The per-hectare peat figures are in line with what the UK peat inventory reports for drained cropland. They are model estimates for a typical Fenland field, not measurements of your fields.

What this could mean for your farm

Options, largest effect first

  1. Raising water levels on the potato ground (about 770–2,100 t CO2e/yr saved in the model). This could be a step-by-step change: keep the water higher in winter and spring under the current crops, or move part of the land to a wet crop (paludiculture such as reed or other wetland crops). It would involve sluices or weirs on your ditches and talking to the internal drainage board. You would also need to think about access, the crops you grow and your markets. Things to look into: Countryside Stewardship and Landscape Recovery options for lowland peat, SFI actions on peat soils, and the Peatland Code, which now covers some lowland peat. Projects under the Peatland Code commit the land for decades.
  2. Feed additive for the cattle (about 59–118 t CO2e/yr saved). This is easiest to apply in winter when the cattle are housed and fed a ration. Check that it suits a suckler system and talk to your vet or nutritionist about cost and practicality. Results can feed into a farm carbon footprint for buyers or processors.
  3. Hedgerow trees (about 0.4–1.0 t CO2/yr on average for 100 trees). This option is small for carbon but useful for shelter, shade for stock and wildlife. Look into SFI hedgerow actions and Countryside Stewardship capital grants for hedgerow trees.

If you are a tenant - Raising water tables or changing land use on the peat is a long-term change to the holding and will almost certainly need your landlord's consent. Check whether your tenancy (FBT or 1986 Act) allows it. - Carbon rights, and any Peatland Code or nature credits, usually belong to the landowner unless your tenancy or a separate agreement says otherwise. Agree who holds and benefits from them before you start. - SFI is open to tenants who have management control for each action's duration and do not breach the tenancy. The Tenant Farming Commissioner's Code of Practice covers landlord–tenant cooperation on schemes and natural capital. - Feed changes and hedgerow trees are usually within a tenant's control. Planting trees may still need your landlord's agreement, depending on the tenancy.

Where to get advice: the Rural Payments Agency and Natural England (schemes), your internal drainage board, Fenland peat and paludiculture projects run by local Wildlife Trusts and others, the Tenant Farmers Association, and a land agent for tenancy and carbon-rights questions.

Possible next steps

  • Install dipwells or share existing water-table readings. The peatland model can then be re-run with your actual water levels instead of the Fenland average.
  • Give the actual follower numbers and housing period so the herd methane and the effect of the additive can be recalculated.
  • Confirm whether the 80 ha of grass is on peat. If it is, model its soil emissions too.
  • Check with your landlord whether a staged water-table trial on one block of the potato ground would be acceptable.

Caveats

  • Not counted: the grass ground's soil (we assumed it is not deep peat; if it is drained peat, its emissions could be large), N2O from livestock manure and grazing, fuel, electricity, and bought-in fertiliser and feed. The real farm total will be higher than 4,217 t CO2e.
  • Peat results use a typical Fenland year and typical drainage (about 0.66 m), not your dipwells. Partial raising under potatoes assumes the water is still drawn down in summer. Whether potatoes can be grown at 0.30–0.45 m on your ground is an agronomic question the model does not answer.
  • The reed scenario charges N2O as if the reed were fertilised like potatoes [R5]. An unfertilised wetland crop would probably give off less N2O, so the reed savings may be somewhat understated. Methane from rewetted land is higher in the first years after rewetting.
  • The herd make-up was assumed: 85 calves, 20 breeding heifers, 40 steers, 20 heifers for slaughter, 2 bulls, 5 rams and 300 lambs. The methane figures use UK inventory average factors per head (Farm Carbon Calculator Tier 2), not your animals' actual weights or diets.
  • Feed-additive effects (15% and 30%) are our assumptions for a mainly grazing suckler herd, not results the model worked out.
  • The hedgerow tree numbers (100 trees on 3 km) are assumed. Growth comes from the Fens pilot tile, not your own hedges. The hedges themselves (below 3 m) are not modelled.
  • Water levels in the Fens are managed across whole areas through drainage boards. Raising them on one block may depend on your neighbours and the internal drainage board, and could affect the land next to you.
  • The digital twins describe typical conditions in the area around the farm; they do not know your fields, herd or records unless you supplied them.
  • This is not financial, legal or agronomic advice. Check scheme eligibility and current payment rates with the Rural Payments Agency or Natural England, and talk to an adviser before changing the business.
  • Tenants: check your tenancy agreement and talk to your landlord before land use change or selling carbon or nature credits.
