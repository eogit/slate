Each persona below asks a question that no single digital twin can answer. The orchestrator splits it up, interrogates the twins it needs over MCP, puts their outputs on a common basis and writes one report for that persona. ## Policy-maker (land use and agricultural policy)

**Question.** For the Land Use Framework we need to compare the greenhouse-gas return per hectare of three land-use changes in East Anglia over the next 25 years: raising the water table on drained Fenland arable peat, planting new woodland on arable land in Breckland, and planting hedgerow and agroforestry trees on Fenland farms. How does each compare per hectare, and how big is livestock methane in the same area by comparison? Which should be prioritised, and how confident can we be?

**Why it needs several twins.** Peat rewetting (DT8), afforestation (DT4) and trees outside woodland (DT6) are modelled by different twins with different units and horizons; livestock methane (DT2) is the benchmark. Only the orchestrator can put them on one per-hectare, per-year basis.

**How the orchestrator answered it.**

- Recorded 6 assumptions before calling any twin.
- DT8 peatland GHG: ghg_balance, water_table_scenario, validate_inventory, ghg_balance
- DT4 forest biomass: afforestation_scenario, afforestation_scenario, validate
- DT6 trees outside woodland: planting_scenario, planting_scenario, validate
- DT2 livestock methane: area_methane, validate_naei
- Figures: 2. Backend: claude-code, 144 s.

**Synthesised answer (key findings).**

- Per hectare, raising the water table on Fen arable peat gives the largest benefit by a wide margin. The model puts drained winter wheat at 37.3 t CO2e/ha/yr (AR6) [R1]. With a reed crop at a mean water table of 0.1–0.2 m, the net balance falls to 0.9–1.7 t CO2e/ha/yr [R2]. That avoids about 36 t CO2e/ha/yr, roughly 890–910 t CO2e/ha over 25 years. If the CO2 response follows the UK Peatland Inventory equation instead, the figure is about 32 t CO2e/ha/yr (our arithmetic) [R9].
- A partial step that keeps wheat but holds the water table at about 0.4 m gives 21.6 t CO2e/ha/yr, a saving of about 16 t CO2e/ha/yr (≈390 t/ha over 25 years) [R12].
- New Corsican pine woodland in Breckland removes about 331–342 t CO2/ha over 25 years (13.2–13.7 t/ha/yr), with a 90 % range of roughly 48–553 t/ha [R3]. Mixed broadleaves remove much less: 80–180 t CO2/ha (3.2–7.2 t/ha/yr) [R4]. Fen rewetting therefore gives about 2.5–3 times the conifer figure per hectare, and 5–11 times the broadleaf figure.
- Fenland agroforestry at an assumed 100 trees/ha removes about 2.6–10.9 t CO2 per hectare of field over 25 years. Hedgerow trees at an assumed 10 per hectare of farm remove about 0.3–1.4 t/ha [R5, R6]. Per hectare this is one to two orders of magnitude below woodland, and the growth model behind it has no hold-out skill in the Fens [R11].
- Livestock methane in the 4,800 km² Fens–Breckland box is modest. DT2 estimates 3,055 t CH4/yr, or 82,500 t CO2e/yr (AR6) [R7]. That is about 0.17 t CO2e per hectare of land per year (our arithmetic). The NAEI map gives 5,569 t CH4 for the same box, about 1.8 times more [R8]. On DT2's figure, rewetting about 2,300 ha of Fen arable to 0.2 m would avoid as much each year as the whole area's livestock emits (about 4,100 ha on the NAEI figure; our arithmetic).
- On greenhouse gases per hectare alone, the order is clear: Fen water-table raising, then conifer woodland, then broadleaf woodland, then farm trees. The evidence is strongest for the size of the peat saving. It is weakest for farm trees and for livestock in this arable-dominated area.

**Policy relevance.**

**Bearing on policy**
- **Land Use Framework spatial priorities:** on greenhouse gases per hectare, the results support treating deep drained Fen arable peat as a top-tier candidate for land-use change.
- **Seventh Carbon Budget:** the CCC's pathway relies on peat restoration and woodland creation, and both options are material contributors here.
- **16.5 % canopy target:** woodland and farm trees contribute to it. Rewetting does not.
- **Schemes:** relevant routes are Landscape Recovery and Countryside Stewardship Higher Tier for rewetting and paludiculture, SFI for agroforestry and hedgerow trees, and woodland creation grants together with the Woodland Carbon Code and Peatland Code.

**Options and trade-offs**
1. **Full rewetting to about 0.1–0.3 m with paludiculture.** Largest saving, available quickly. Ends conventional cropping on high-grade land and needs catchment-level water management. Many Fen farms are tenanted, so landlord consent and the allocation of carbon rights would matter.
2. **Partial water-table raising (about 0.4 m) under arable.** About half the saving while cropping continues. Could suit a broader uptake approach.
3. **Breckland woodland.** Removals build up and last longer. Conifers deliver about 2–4 times the broadleaf figure on these soils. The choice involves trade-offs with heathland and other habitat, which these agents do not assess.
4. **Farm trees.** Small greenhouse-gas effect per hectare; their justification lies in co-benefits that are not modelled here.
5. **Livestock measures.** Limited leverage in this area relative to peat.

**Strength of evidence**
- **Peat saving:** calibrated and checked against UK Peatland Inventory Tier 2 factors (good agreement for cropland CO2 and rewetted CH4). The model is more optimistic than the inventory's CO2 equation at intermediate water tables. Rated: calibrated, moderate to high confidence in the size and ranking, lower in the exact optimum.
- **Woodland:** calibrated to satellite biomass, with hold-out R² of 0.63–0.77. Rated: validated for existing stands, indicative for new planting on arable.
- **Farm trees:** rated indicative only, as there is no hold-out skill.
- **Livestock in East Anglia:** rated indicative, since it is about half the NAEI value.

**What would change the conclusion**
- Field or eddy-covariance data from rewetted Fen sites, to confirm CO2 at 0.2–0.4 m water tables.
- Mapping of peat depth and how much is left.
- Soil-carbon change under new Breckland woodland.
- Food-output and leakage analysis.
- A longer (50-year) horizon, which would favour woodland relatively.

## Farmer (owner-occupier or tenant) or land manager

**Question.** I'm a tenant farmer near Ely on 180 ha: 60 ha of potatoes and 40 ha of winter wheat on deep peat, 80 ha of grass, 90 suckler cows with followers and 200 breeding ewes, and a few kilometres of hedges. Where do most of my farm's emissions come from, and what would raising the water table on the potato ground, a methane-reducing feed additive and planting hedgerow trees each do?

**Why it needs several twins.** The peat soils (DT8), the herd (DT2) and the farm's trees (DT6) are three different twins; the farmer wants one ranked list of sources and options.

**How the orchestrator answered it.**

- Recorded 8 assumptions before calling any twin.
- DT8 peatland GHG: ghg_balance, ghg_balance, ghg_balance, ghg_balance, water_table_scenario
- DT2 livestock methane: farm_methane, farm_methane, farm_methane
- DT6 trees outside woodland: planting_scenario
- Figures: 2. Backend: claude-code, 117 s.

**Synthesised answer (key findings).**

- The drained peat is by far the biggest source. The 100 ha of potatoes and wheat give off about 3,710 t CO2e a year: about 37 t CO2e per hectare, mostly CO2 from the peat breaking down [R1, R2]. That is roughly 88% of the farm total we could model.
- Methane from the livestock comes to about 504 t CO2e a year (18.7 t of methane). About 87% of that comes from the cattle and 13% from the sheep. Nearly all of it comes from digestion (enteric methane) rather than manure [R6].
- Raising the water table on the 60 ha of potatoes is the biggest lever. If you keep growing potatoes, raising the yearly average water table from about 0.66 m to 0.45 m below the surface cuts about 770 t CO2e a year, and raising it to 0.30 m cuts about 1,410 t. Rewetting the land to grow reed at about 0.20 m cuts about 2,100 t a year [R1, R3–R5].
- A feed additive fed to the cattle cuts about 59 t CO2e a year if it works out at a 15% herd-average reduction, or about 118 t at 30%. That is about 1.5–3% of the farm total [R6–R8].
- 100 new hedgerow trees would take up on average about 0.4–1.0 t CO2 a year over 50 years, or 18–48 t in total over that time [R9]. That is small for climate, but trees can do other jobs on the farm.

**What this could mean for your farm.**

**Options, largest effect first**

1. **Raising water levels on the potato ground** (about 770–2,100 t CO2e/yr saved in the model). This could be a step-by-step change: keep the water higher in winter and spring under the current crops, or move part of the land to a wet crop (paludiculture such as reed or other wetland crops). It would involve sluices or weirs on your ditches and talking to the internal drainage board. You would also need to think about access, the crops you grow and your markets. Things to look into: Countryside Stewardship and Landscape Recovery options for lowland peat, SFI actions on peat soils, and the **Peatland Code**, which now covers some lowland peat. Projects under the Peatland Code commit the land for decades.
2. **Feed additive for the cattle** (about 59–118 t CO2e/yr saved). This is easiest to apply in winter when the cattle are housed and fed a ration. Check that it suits a suckler system and talk to your vet or nutritionist about cost and practicality. Results can feed into a farm carbon footprint for buyers or processors.
3. **Hedgerow trees** (about 0.4–1.0 t CO2/yr on average for 100 trees). This option is small for carbon but useful for shelter, shade for stock and wildlife. Look into SFI hedgerow actions and Countryside Stewardship capital grants for hedgerow trees.

**If you are a tenant**
- Raising water tables or changing land use on the peat is a long-term change to the holding and will almost certainly need your landlord's consent. Check whether your tenancy (FBT or 1986 Act) allows it.
- Carbon rights, and any Peatland Code or nature credits, usually belong to the landowner unless your tenancy or a separate agreement says otherwise. Agree who holds and benefits from them **before** you start.
- SFI is open to tenants who have management control for each action's duration and do not breach the tenancy. The Tenant Farming Commissioner's Code of Practice covers landlord–tenant cooperation on schemes and natural capital.
- Feed changes and hedgerow trees are usually within a tenant's control. Planting trees may still need your landlord's agreement, depending on the tenancy.

**Where to get advice:** the Rural Payments Agency and Natural England (schemes), your internal drainage board, Fenland peat and paludiculture projects run by local Wildlife Trusts and others, the Tenant Farmers Association, and a land agent for tenancy and carbon-rights questions.

## Green finance manager (carbon and nature credits, tokenisation)

**Question.** We are building a portfolio of UK nature-based carbon projects that we may later tokenise. For East Anglia, compare the expected abatement per hectare and its uncertainty for a Peatland Code rewetting project on Fenland cropland, a new Corsican pine woodland near Thetford of the kind registered under the Woodland Carbon Code, and hedgerow agroforestry on Fenland farms. How well validated is each model, and which project type is the most robust basis for credits?

**Why it needs several twins.** Each project type sits in a different twin, and each twin has its own validation evidence (inventory comparison, CCI hold-out, lidar hold-out). The credit question is really about comparing those uncertainties side by side.

**How the orchestrator answered it.**

- Recorded 5 assumptions before calling any twin.
- DT8 peatland GHG: ghg_balance, ghg_balance, water_table_scenario, validate_inventory
- DT4 forest biomass: afforestation_scenario, afforestation_scenario, validate
- DT6 trees outside woodland: planting_scenario, planting_scenario, validate
- Figures: 1. Backend: claude-code, 134 s.

**Synthesised answer (key findings).**

- Peat rewetting gives by far the most abatement per hectare. Drained Fen cropland emits about 37 t CO2e/ha/yr (winter wheat 37.3, potato 37.0, AR6 GWP100) [R1, R2]. Rewetted reed fen emits 0.9–2.5 t CO2e/ha/yr at a water table 0.05–0.2 m below the surface [R3]. That is roughly 34–36 t CO2e/ha/yr of avoided emissions (derived), which starts in year one.
- New Corsican pine at Thetford removes an average of 16.5–20.2 t CO2/ha/yr over 50 years with no thinning, depending on the growth model. The 90 % range is very wide, roughly 3–33 t/ha/yr (derived from 50-year totals of −1,010 t/ha [−1,653 to −145] and −824 t/ha [−1,310 to −154]) [R5]. With thinning and felling the average drops to 10.9–11.9 t/ha/yr [R6]. Removals start slowly: under 5 t/ha/yr in the first 6 to 7 years.
- Hedgerow and agroforestry trees on Fenland farms add little per hectare. At an assumed 100 trees/ha they remove about 0.3–1.0 t CO2/ha/yr over 50 years (derived from 1,000-tree runs: hedgerow −182 to −481 t, agroforestry −142 to −373 t in total) [R8, R9]. They also do nothing about the roughly 37 t CO2e/ha/yr the drained peat underneath keeps emitting.
- Validation strength differs by type. The woodland model was tested on later years it was not fitted to (2018–2024), against satellite biomass maps (ESA Biomass CCI v7): it explains about 63–66 % of the variation (R² 0.63–0.66), with an error of about 36–37 Mg/ha (RMSE) and a small overestimate on average (bias +1.7 to +2.4 Mg/ha) [R7]. The peat model matches the UK Peatland Inventory's standard (Tier-2) emission factors closely, for example rewetted-fen CH4 109 vs 111 kg/ha/yr [R4], but this is a check against the inventory, not against independent flux measurements. The Fenland tree model does no better than assuming the trees stop growing (skill −0.0002 and −0.35) and predicts 2.3–6 times the height growth seen in lidar [R10].
- On this evidence, peat rewetting is the most robust basis for credits: it has the largest abatement, a tight model range and agreement with the inventory. Its main risks are whether the target water table is actually reached and kept, and the lack of an uncertainty ensemble in the model. Thetford pine comes second: its validation is independent but its spread is wide. Fenland hedgerow and agroforestry trees are not a robust credit basis on these models.

**Credit and market relevance.**

**These are model estimates, not creditable units.** Under the Peatland Code and the WCC, claimable quantities come from the code's own methods:
- Peatland Code: emission-factor tables by condition category and a code risk buffer.
- WCC: the Carbon Calculation Spreadsheet, a baseline deduction, a leakage check and a buffer.

Units are listed on the UK Land Carbon Registry as Pending Issuance Units. They become verified units only after independent verification. The figures here could support due diligence and monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV), but they do not replace a code calculation.

**Integrity points the results speak to**

*Baseline*
- Peat: the drained-cropland baseline (about 37 t CO2e/ha/yr) matches inventory cropland factors, which makes it defensible [R1, R4].
- Woodland: the runs start from bare land and omit soil carbon and baseline vegetation. Code baselines would reduce the net figure.

*Permanence and reversal*
- Peat: reversal happens through re-drainage or drought. Re-drying to 0.5 m brings emissions back to about 16 t CO2e/ha/yr [R3], so water-level control and neighbouring drainage are the main risks.
- Woodland: risks are fire, wind and pests on Breckland. The runs used no stochastic disturbance, so this is not quantified here. Thinning cuts mean in-forest removals by about 40–45 % [R5, R6].

*Leakage*
- Taking high-value Fen cropland out of production is a material leakage and food-production issue. These models do not quantify it.

*Uncertainty*
- Peat: no ensemble range (deterministic run). Treat 34–36 t CO2e/ha/yr as a sensitivity range, not a confidence interval.
- Woodland: 90 % ranges are very wide, so issuance planned on the mean is exposed to overestimation.
- Fenland trees: unvalidated.

*MRV*
- Peat: the measurable state variable (water table) maps directly onto the abatement, a strong basis for digital MRV (dMRV).
- Woodland: satellite biomass (CCI) saturates at about 150 Mg/ha. Mature-stand stock would need lidar or plot data to verify.
- Fenland trees: lidar heights exist, but the model does not yet track them.

**Tokenisation**
- What the twin can provide: repeatable, provenance-logged model runs and observation-based state variables (water table, satellite and lidar biomass) that a token's metadata could reference.
- What it cannot establish: legal title, carbon rights, registry status or regulatory classification.
- Any token should link one-to-one to a specific UK Land Carbon Registry unit (PIU or verified). It must not be issued against these model estimates, and retirement must be mirrored on the registry to avoid double counting.
- Whether a tokenised structure falls within FCA regulation (cryptoasset regime from 25 October 2027) depends on how it is structured and needs legal review.

**Claims and disclosure**
- Peat rewetting is avoided emissions (an emission reduction). Woodland is a removal. Buyers' claims under the VCMI Claims Code and the forthcoming UK guidance (spring 2027) differ by type, so the portfolio should be labelled accordingly.
- WCC is under ICVCM Core Carbon Principles assessment but not approved.
- Disclose model uncertainty under TNFD and ISSB/UK SRS so that any claims meet the FCA anti-greenwashing rule.

This is not investment advice, and no prices or returns are implied.

## Research scientist

**Question.** Across the SLATE twins, how good is each model at reproducing independent observations, and where is the structural uncertainty largest? Compare the hold-out skill of the forest biomass models at Thetford, of the tree-height models for trees outside woodland in Breckland and the Fens against persistence, of the livestock methane map against the NAEI over Norfolk and Suffolk, and of the peatland model against the UK Peatland Inventory emission factors.

**Why it needs several twins.** Each twin validates against a different observing system (Biomass CCI, EA lidar, NAEI, the Peatland Inventory). A cross-twin skill comparison needs all four validation tools and a common reading of their metrics.

**How the orchestrator answered it.**

- Recorded 5 assumptions before calling any twin.
- DT4 forest biomass: validate
- DT6 trees outside woodland: validate
- DT2 livestock methane: validate_naei, calibration_report
- DT8 peatland GHG: validate_inventory
- Figures: 3. Backend: claude-code, 118 s.

**Synthesised answer (key findings).**

- DT4 Thetford: hold-out R² against CCI v7 ranges from −0.03 to 0.77 (RMSE 28.8–63.8 Mg ha⁻¹). Skill is best for mixed broadleaves (3-PG R² 0.77) and Corsican pine (0.63–0.66). Letting stand age vary freely raises R² for Scots pine, beech and oak from 0.12–0.30 to 0.51–0.57, so errors in inventory ages matter about as much as the choice of model [R1].
- The two DT4 models disagree most for beech and oak. 3-PG is biased by −29.5 and −28.6 Mg ha⁻¹; the yield curve by −4.7 and +7.0 Mg ha⁻¹. Both models predict steady AGB gains over 2018–2024, but the CCI landscape mean is roughly flat (Corsican pine: 115.7→115.0 Mg ha⁻¹ observed, 112.5→121.9 yield curve) [R1].
- DT6: in Breckland (forecast horizon about 9 years) both models beat persistence (skill 0.50 size_age, 0.47 crown_lue). In the Fens (horizon about 1.4 years) they do not (−0.0002 and −0.35). crown_lue predicts 2.03 m yr⁻¹ of growth against 0.33 m yr⁻¹ observed, the largest model-structure discrepancy found in this comparison [R2].
- DT2 vs NAEI over the Norfolk–Suffolk box: r = 0.39 at 1 km, rising to 0.68 at 20 km, and DT2/NAEI totals = 0.61. The England hold-out gives r = 0.65 at 1 km, 0.97 at 20 km and a ratio of 0.89. East Anglia is therefore one of the regions DT2 reproduces worst [R3, R4].
- DT8 vs UK Peatland Inventory: cropland CO2 is close (27.7 vs 27.1 t CO2 ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹), and so is rewetted-fen CH4 (109.3 vs 111.4 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹). Across the Tier-2 classes CH4 scores NSE = 0.83. However, CH4 for grassland on peat is 6–10× too low (5.8 vs 35.9 and 2.8 vs 29.0 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹), and at 20–30 cm water tables CO2 is 4.6–7.2 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ below the inventory's Eq. 1 [R5].

**Methods, uncertainty and reproducibility.**

**Models and data.**
- **DT4 0.2.0** [R1]: yield curves and 3-PG, calibrated pixel by pixel on ESA Biomass CCI v7.0 (100 m; Santoro & Cartus 2026, CEDA catalogue uuid 6429d1aa…). Woodland mask from NFI 2024; ages and species from the Forestry England sub-compartment database; 3-PG climate from HadUK-Grid 1991–2020 with Hargreaves–Samani radiation. Parameters are the shipped calibration (scripts/calibrate_pilots.py). Up to 2000 sample pixels per species group; each pixel is treated as an unthinned even-aged stand.
- **DT6 0.1.0** [R2]: size_age and crown_lue models on the National Trees Outside Woodland map V1 and EA lidar DSM/DTM since 1998, with Jucker et al. (2017) allometry. Hold-out: latest epoch, two-fold pilot prior.
- **DT2** [R3, R4]: June Survey 2020 livestock allocated to 1 km. Configuration chosen by grid search (alpha_other 0, block 10 km, ACN weight 0.5), ranked by r at 1 km on a 20 km checkerboard and scored on the complementary squares. Default site, no NAEI assimilation.
- **DT8 0.1.0** [R5]: PIGNN CO2 and N2O models and a CH4 model with a flood-litter pool, all on calibrated baseline parameters. Reference: Evans et al. (2023), Eq. 1 and Eq. 3, Fig. 3.9 and the Tier-2 emission factors. GWP100 AR6 (CH4 27, N2O 273), although no CO2e figures are used here.

**Calibration vs validation.** Only the DT4 and DT6 results are true out-of-sample tests against observations. The DT2 England hold-out is out-of-sample spatially but not in its reference dataset. Calibration r (0.663) and hold-out r (0.653) are close, so overfitting of the three allocation hyperparameters is small. The DT8 comparison is a consistency check against an empirical synthesis.

**Dominant uncertainties.**
- DT4: stand age (free-age R² gains up to 0.45) and CCI saturation; model structure only for broadleaves.
- DT6: the growth formulation of crown_lue, and the horizon.
- DT2: non-ruminant sources in the reference, and the allocation proxy in arable regions.
- DT8: the shape of the CH4 response at 30–45 cm and the CO2 response at 20–30 cm.

**Reproducibility.** The calls were dt4__validate(landscapes=[thetford]), dt6__validate(pilots=[breckland, fens]), dt2__validate_naei(bbox_bng=[555000,215000,655000,345000]), dt2__calibration_report() and dt8__validate_inventory(site=default). All use shipped parameters and are deterministic.

**Suggested tests.**
- **DT4:** repeat the hold-out with GEDI L4A or EA lidar-derived AGB to separate CCI saturation from model error. Test 3-PG broadleaf parameters (e.g. the allocation and mortality terms for BE and OK) against FE yield class.
- **DT6:** rerun the Fens validation with a longer gap between epochs, or with matched horizons across pilots. Constrain crown_lue's light-use efficiency using the Breckland growth rates.
- **DT2:** subtract the NAEI pig, poultry, horse and deer components, or compare against NAEI cattle and sheep sub-sectors if available, over East Anglia. Compute r on county polygons.
- **DT8:** assimilate chamber or eddy-covariance CH4 from Fenland grassland sites at 30–45 cm water tables with dt8__assimilate_ch4, and test whether ditch CH4 explains the gap to the Tier-2 grassland factors.

## NGO (conservation, nature restoration and environmental charities)

**Question.** We are a conservation charity working in Breckland. How much carbon is held in the hedgerow and field trees outside the forest, what would ash dieback do to it and how much would replanting recover? How has the biomass of Thetford Forest changed since 2015, and how much grassland and grazing livestock is there in the same area for heathland and grassland restoration?

**Why it needs several twins.** Trees outside woodland (DT6), the forest (DT4) and the grazing landscape (DT2) are the three parts of the Breckland mosaic the charity cares about.

**How the orchestrator answered it.**

- Recorded 4 assumptions before calling any twin.
- DT6 trees outside woodland: carbon_stock, tree_inventory, ash_dieback_scenario, ash_dieback_scenario, ash_dieback_scenario
- DT4 forest biomass: biomass_change, agb_timeseries
- DT2 livestock methane: area_methane, grassland_map
- Figures: 2. Backend: claude-code, 110 s.

**Synthesised answer (key findings).**

- Trees outside woodland in the 25 km² Breckland pilot tile (3,727 trees: lone trees, groups and small woods) hold about 925 t of living carbon (3,390 t CO2), 90% range about 590–1,260 t C from 937–1,998 t of above-ground biomass. That is only 0.37 t C per hectare of land, because tree canopy covers just 1.1% of the tile [R1][R2].
- Ash dieback (15% of broadleaves are ash, 80% of them die) would leave about 916 t C in living trees by 2055, against about 1,000 t C with no dieback. The gap is about 84 t C, roughly 8% (our arithmetic). Over 30 years the trees would take up about 6,940 t CO2 instead of about 7,640 t CO2, roughly 700 t CO2 less [R3].
- Replanting every dead ash tree would put back only about 8 t C by 2055, about a tenth of the loss. Replanting half of them would put back about 4 t C. This is because young trees grow slowly for their first decades. Replanting is a long-term recovery, not a quick one [R4][R5].
- Thetford Forest's mean above-ground biomass fell from 108.9 Mg/ha in 2015 to 102.9 Mg/ha in 2024, a change of −6.0 Mg/ha with a 90% range of −33.1 to +21.2. This is not statistically significant. Over the same years 1,009 ha lost biomass significantly and 493 ha gained [R6][R7].
- Within 15 km of central Breckland (706 km²), Living England maps 21,598 ha of acid, calcareous and neutral grassland, 2,591 ha of dwarf shrub heath and 1,107 ha of improved grassland. Grazing stock is light at 0.21 livestock units per hectare of grazing land: about 20,300 sheep, 3,300 beef and other cattle and 1,900 dairy cattle [R8][R9].

**Nature recovery and advocacy relevance.**

**Restoration choices.** These results support a "better, more joined-up" approach to field and hedgerow trees. The existing stock is small, about 925 t C in the pilot tile, and ash dieback could cost about 84 t C of growth by 2055. Replanting recovers only about a tenth of that by then. So protecting surviving and tolerant trees, and leaving dead ash as standing or fallen deadwood where safe, keeps more carbon on site than replanting alone. Replanting earns its value over longer horizons. For heathland and grassland restoration, the mapped resource of about 21,600 ha of semi-natural grassland and 2,600 ha of dwarf shrub heath is large, and current stocking is light (0.21 livestock units per hectare). How suitable a site is for restoration grazing depends on habitat condition and stock type, which these twins do not assess.

**What was and was not quantified.** The twins quantified tree biomass and carbon, satellite-observed forest biomass change, livestock methane and mapped habitat area. They did **not** model species, heathland condition, invertebrates or birds such as stone curlew and nightjar, water, or soil carbon. They also did not model the trade-off between conifer biomass and open-habitat restoration, for example the carbon cost of clearing conifers back to heath. The DT4 tools could estimate that if you need it.

**Statements suitable for public use (with the stated qualifiers):**
- "Trees outside woodland in a 25 km² Breckland sample hold about 925 tonnes of carbon (model estimate, range about 590–1,260 t)."
- "Modelling suggests ash dieback could reduce this by around 8% by 2055, and replanting would recover only a small part within 30 years." Qualify this as a scenario using an assumed 15% ash share.
- "Satellite data show no statistically significant change in Thetford Forest's average biomass between 2015 and 2024." Avoid saying the forest is "losing carbon".

**Statements that need care:** do not present the 21,600 ha as heath or as priority grassland in good condition. Do not attribute the 1,009 ha of biomass loss to any cause without felling or disturbance records.

**Monitoring.** Repeat lidar from the Environment Agency, or drone surveys of tree heights, can be assimilated into DT6 to track real ash mortality and regrowth. Recording the local ash share would make the scenario much more reliable. Annual CCI maps, combined with felling licence data, would let DT4 attribute biomass loss at Thetford.

## General public

**Question.** I live in the Fens. Which matters more for the climate here: the drained peat fields, the cattle and sheep, or the trees in hedges and around farms? What could change it?

**Why it needs several twins.** A plain question that spans three twins; the answer has to put soil, livestock and trees on one scale without jargon.

**How the orchestrator answered it.**

- Recorded 7 assumptions before calling any twin.
- DT6 trees outside woodland: list_landscapes, carbon_stock, simulate_trees, planting_scenario
- DT8 peatland GHG: ghg_balance, ghg_balance, ghg_balance, water_table_scenario, validate_inventory, ghg_balance
- DT2 livestock methane: area_methane, area_methane, grassland_map, methane_scenario, methane_scenario
- Figures: 2. Backend: claude-code, 111 s.

**Synthesised answer (key findings).**

- Each hectare of drained peat farmland gives off about 23–37 t CO2e a year, depending on the crop (sugar beet 23.2, potato 37.0, winter wheat 37.3; AR6 GWP100). Most of this is CO2 from the peat slowly breaking down [R2–R4].
- All the cattle and sheep in the 25 km² pilot square together produce about 24 t of methane a year, or 640 t CO2e (AR6) [R6]. Simple arithmetic: just 17–28 ha of drained peat cropland gives off as much as all those animals.
- The trees outside woodland in the square (about 2,400 trees, mostly willows, poplars and trees around farmsteads) hold about 1,290 t CO2 in living wood. They are modelled to take up roughly 110–120 t CO2 a year [R8, R9]. That offsets about 3–5 ha of drained peat.
- Raising the water table is by far the biggest lever. In the model, keeping water about 0.2 m below the surface (for example with wet farming such as reed) cuts the net balance to about 2.0 t CO2e/ha/yr, saving roughly 35 t CO2e per hectare each year [R5, R2].
- Livestock options have much smaller effects at this scale. A 20% cut in cattle enteric methane (from a feed additive) saves about 97 t CO2e/yr across the whole square, and 25% fewer animals saves about 160 t CO2e/yr (AR6) [R12, R13]. Planting 1,000 new hedgerow trees takes up about 53 t CO2 over 30 years in total (range 22–104 t) [R14].
