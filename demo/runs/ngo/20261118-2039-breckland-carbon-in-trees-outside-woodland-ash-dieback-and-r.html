Breckland: carbon in trees outside woodland, ash dieback and replanting, Thetford Forest biomass since 2015, and grassland and grazing livestock

Prepared for: A conservation charity working in Breckland (reserve, restoration and policy staff) (persona: NGO (conservation, nature restoration and environmental charities)) · 18 November 2026 · SLATE digital-twin orchestrator

Question. We are a conservation charity working in Breckland. How much carbon is held in the hedgerow and field trees outside the forest, what would ash dieback do to it and how much would replanting recover? How has the biomass of Thetford Forest changed since 2015, and how much grassland and grazing livestock is there in the same area for heathland and grassland restoration?

As understood. (1) How much living carbon is held in hedgerow, field and other trees outside woodland in Breckland; (2) what ash dieback would do to it and how much replanting dead ash would recover; (3) how the above-ground biomass of Thetford Forest has changed since 2015; and (4) how much grassland, heath and grazing livestock there is in the same area, as context for heathland and grassland restoration. Trees outside woodland are answered for the DT6 Breckland 5 km pilot tile only; Thetford Forest for the DT4 Thetford landscape; grassland and livestock for a 15 km radius around 52.45 N, 0.70 E.

Key findings

  • Trees outside woodland in the 25 km² Breckland pilot tile (3,727 trees: lone trees, groups and small woods) hold about 925 t of living carbon (3,390 t CO2), 90% range about 590–1,260 t C from 937–1,998 t of above-ground biomass. That is only 0.37 t C per hectare of land, because tree canopy covers just 1.1% of the tile [R1][R2].
  • Ash dieback (15% of broadleaves are ash, 80% of them die) would leave about 916 t C in living trees by 2055, against about 1,000 t C with no dieback. The gap is about 84 t C, roughly 8% (our arithmetic). Over 30 years the trees would take up about 6,940 t CO2 instead of about 7,640 t CO2, roughly 700 t CO2 less [R3].
  • Replanting every dead ash tree would put back only about 8 t C by 2055, about a tenth of the loss. Replanting half of them would put back about 4 t C. This is because young trees grow slowly for their first decades. Replanting is a long-term recovery, not a quick one [R4][R5].
  • Thetford Forest's mean above-ground biomass fell from 108.9 Mg/ha in 2015 to 102.9 Mg/ha in 2024, a change of −6.0 Mg/ha with a 90% range of −33.1 to +21.2. This is not statistically significant. Over the same years 1,009 ha lost biomass significantly and 493 ha gained [R6][R7].
  • Within 15 km of central Breckland (706 km²), Living England maps 21,598 ha of acid, calcareous and neutral grassland, 2,591 ha of dwarf shrub heath and 1,107 ha of improved grassland. Grazing stock is light at 0.21 livestock units per hectare of grazing land: about 20,300 sheep, 3,300 beef and other cattle and 1,900 dairy cattle [R8][R9].

3. Thetford Forest above-ground biomass (DT4, ESA Biomass CCI v7, 13,670 ha of woodland)

  • Mean AGB went from 108.9 Mg/ha (90% range 91.5–126.2) in 2015 to 102.9 Mg/ha (82.8–123.0) in 2024. The 2015–2024 trend is −0.57 Mg/ha per year [R7].
  • The change from 2015 to 2024 is −6.0 ± 16.5 Mg/ha (90% range −33.1 to +21.2). It is not significant at 90%, so the EBCF impact is "no detectable change" [R6].
  • Pixels with significant loss cover 1,009 ha. Of these, 566 ha are candidates for conversion (for example clearfell) and 443 ha are modification (partial loss). Pixels with significant gain cover 493 ha [R6]. No cause is attributed, because no felling, storm or other evidence was supplied.
  • For scale only: −6.0 Mg/ha over 13,670 ha is about −82,000 t of AGB (our arithmetic). Its uncertainty range includes zero.

What this means

Trees outside woodland: a small carbon store, but vulnerable. In this open, arable-and-heath landscape, hedgerow and field trees hold under 1 t C per hectare. That is because tree cover is only about 1%. Their value is probably more about structure, connectivity and habitat than about carbon. A large share of their biomass sits in lone trees, which are hard to replace quickly.

Ash dieback would cost more in lost growth than in standing stock. Under the default assumptions, living carbon dips by about 33 t C over the first decade, from 921 to 888 t C. The bigger effect is that the dead ash stop growing. By 2055 the tile holds about 84 t C less than it would without dieback, and it takes up about 700 t CO2 less over 30 years. Part of the carbon in dead trees stays on site as deadwood, which is also a habitat resource, as long as dead trees are left rather than burnt as firewood.

Replanting is worthwhile for the future, but it will not restore the carbon within a generation. Even replacing every dead ash tree recovers only about 8 t C by 2055, a little under a tenth of the gap. The value of replanting lies beyond 2055, and in keeping trees in the landscape at all. Looking after the surviving and tolerant trees protects more carbon in the short term than new planting can add.

Thetford Forest: a small downward drift, not a proven loss. The satellite record suggests a gentle decline since 2015, about 0.6 Mg/ha per year. This is consistent with rotational clearfelling, in which some areas are felled while others regrow (1,009 ha of loss against 493 ha of gain). However, the change across the whole forest is within the measurement uncertainty. CCI biomass also saturates at about 150 Mg/ha, so growth in mature stands may be under-recorded. The causes cannot be named without felling or disturbance records.

Grassland and grazing: a large semi-natural resource with light grazing. The 15 km area contains about 21,600 ha mapped as acid, calcareous or neutral grassland and about 2,600 ha of dwarf shrub heath. Stocking averages 0.21 livestock units per hectare, mostly sheep and beef cattle, plus some dairy cattle. This gives a first view of where grazing is available for heath and grass-heath management. The grassland class is broad and probably includes grass-heath, some of which may be in poor condition.

Nature recovery and advocacy relevance

Restoration choices. These results support a "better, more joined-up" approach to field and hedgerow trees. The existing stock is small, about 925 t C in the pilot tile, and ash dieback could cost about 84 t C of growth by 2055. Replanting recovers only about a tenth of that by then. So protecting surviving and tolerant trees, and leaving dead ash as standing or fallen deadwood where safe, keeps more carbon on site than replanting alone. Replanting earns its value over longer horizons. For heathland and grassland restoration, the mapped resource of about 21,600 ha of semi-natural grassland and 2,600 ha of dwarf shrub heath is large, and current stocking is light (0.21 livestock units per hectare). How suitable a site is for restoration grazing depends on habitat condition and stock type, which these twins do not assess.

What was and was not quantified. The twins quantified tree biomass and carbon, satellite-observed forest biomass change, livestock methane and mapped habitat area. They did not model species, heathland condition, invertebrates or birds such as stone curlew and nightjar, water, or soil carbon. They also did not model the trade-off between conifer biomass and open-habitat restoration, for example the carbon cost of clearing conifers back to heath. The DT4 tools could estimate that if you need it.

Statements suitable for public use (with the stated qualifiers): - "Trees outside woodland in a 25 km² Breckland sample hold about 925 tonnes of carbon (model estimate, range about 590–1,260 t)." - "Modelling suggests ash dieback could reduce this by around 8% by 2055, and replanting would recover only a small part within 30 years." Qualify this as a scenario using an assumed 15% ash share. - "Satellite data show no statistically significant change in Thetford Forest's average biomass between 2015 and 2024." Avoid saying the forest is "losing carbon".

Statements that need care: do not present the 21,600 ha as heath or as priority grassland in good condition. Do not attribute the 1,009 ha of biomass loss to any cause without felling or disturbance records.

Monitoring. Repeat lidar from the Environment Agency, or drone surveys of tree heights, can be assimilated into DT6 to track real ash mortality and regrowth. Recording the local ash share would make the scenario much more reliable. Annual CCI maps, combined with felling licence data, would let DT4 attribute biomass loss at Thetford.

Possible next steps

  • Provide a local ash share or tree survey so that DT6 reruns the dieback scenario with Breckland values, and compare both growth models.
  • Supply felling licence or storm footprints so that DT4 can attribute the 1,009 ha of biomass loss at Thetford.
  • Run a DT4 scenario on the carbon consequences of converting selected conifer compartments back to open heath, if that is a restoration option.
  • Run DT2 grazing scenarios (stocking caps, herd changes) for a defined restoration area.

Caveats

  • Trees outside woodland are modelled only for the 25 km² DT6 Breckland pilot tile, not for all of Breckland. Scaling up by area would be indicative only.
  • DT6 covers trees with canopy above 3 m outside National Forest Inventory woodland. Hedges below 3 m are excluded, so carbon in hedgerows themselves is underestimated. Trees removed before the TOW map was made are not in the inventory.
  • No open map gives the ash share in Breckland. The 15% ash share, 80% mortality and 10-year time constant are model defaults, and results scale roughly with the ash share. Only the size-age growth model was run.
  • Thetford biomass is from ESA Biomass CCI v7 at 100 m. It saturates at about 150 Mg/ha, the 2015–2024 change is not significant at 90%, and no cause was attributed because no felling or disturbance evidence was supplied.
  • The 15 km circle for grassland and livestock only approximates 'the same area'. It is not the DT6 tile or the exact Thetford landscape boundary. Livestock numbers are 2020 June Survey totals allocated to 1 km cells by a statistical model, not actual farm counts.
  • Living England's grassland class mixes acid, calcareous and neutral grassland and says nothing about habitat condition. No biodiversity, species, water or soil outcomes were modelled, and soil carbon change under restoration is not covered.
  • The digital twins model greenhouse gases and biomass carbon; they do not assess species, habitat condition or other biodiversity outcomes, so any such benefit is outside this report's evidence.
  • Quote model results as estimates with their ranges and assumptions; they are not monitoring data and should not be presented as observed outcomes.
  • Policy context reflects the position in November 2026.
