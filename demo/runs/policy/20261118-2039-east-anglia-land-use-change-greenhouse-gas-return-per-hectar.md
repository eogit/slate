# East Anglia land-use change: greenhouse-gas return per hectare over 25 years, compared with livestock methane

*Prepared for: Defra Land Use Framework policy team (persona: Policy-maker (land use and agricultural policy)) · 18 November 2026 · SLATE digital-twin orchestrator*

**Question.** For the Land Use Framework we need to compare the greenhouse-gas return per hectare of three land-use changes in East Anglia over the next 25 years: raising the water table on drained Fenland arable peat, planting new woodland on arable land in Breckland, and planting hedgerow and agroforestry trees on Fenland farms. How does each compare per hectare, and how big is livestock methane in the same area by comparison? Which should be prioritised, and how confident can we be?

**As understood.** Compare the modelled greenhouse-gas benefit per hectare over 2026–2050 of three land-use changes in East Anglia: (1) raising the water table on drained Fenland arable peat, (2) new woodland on arable land in Breckland, and (3) hedgerow and agroforestry trees on Fenland farms. Set these against livestock methane in the same area, and say which looks the strongest candidate for priority and how confident we can be. Rewetting is compared with drained winter wheat. Woodland and tree planting are compared with bare arable land, counting tree carbon only. Livestock methane is reported for an 80 km × 60 km box covering the Fens and Breckland.

## Key findings

- Per hectare, raising the water table on Fen arable peat gives the largest benefit by a wide margin. The model puts drained winter wheat at 37.3 t CO2e/ha/yr (AR6) [R1]. With a reed crop at a mean water table of 0.1–0.2 m, the net balance falls to 0.9–1.7 t CO2e/ha/yr [R2]. That avoids about 36 t CO2e/ha/yr, roughly 890–910 t CO2e/ha over 25 years. If the CO2 response follows the UK Peatland Inventory equation instead, the figure is about 32 t CO2e/ha/yr (our arithmetic) [R9].
- A partial step that keeps wheat but holds the water table at about 0.4 m gives 21.6 t CO2e/ha/yr, a saving of about 16 t CO2e/ha/yr (≈390 t/ha over 25 years) [R12].
- New Corsican pine woodland in Breckland removes about 331–342 t CO2/ha over 25 years (13.2–13.7 t/ha/yr), with a 90 % range of roughly 48–553 t/ha [R3]. Mixed broadleaves remove much less: 80–180 t CO2/ha (3.2–7.2 t/ha/yr) [R4]. Fen rewetting therefore gives about 2.5–3 times the conifer figure per hectare, and 5–11 times the broadleaf figure.
- Fenland agroforestry at an assumed 100 trees/ha removes about 2.6–10.9 t CO2 per hectare of field over 25 years. Hedgerow trees at an assumed 10 per hectare of farm remove about 0.3–1.4 t/ha [R5, R6]. Per hectare this is one to two orders of magnitude below woodland, and the growth model behind it has no hold-out skill in the Fens [R11].
- Livestock methane in the 4,800 km² Fens–Breckland box is modest. DT2 estimates 3,055 t CH4/yr, or 82,500 t CO2e/yr (AR6) [R7]. That is about 0.17 t CO2e per hectare of land per year (our arithmetic). The NAEI map gives 5,569 t CH4 for the same box, about 1.8 times more [R8]. On DT2's figure, rewetting about 2,300 ha of Fen arable to 0.2 m would avoid as much each year as the whole area's livestock emits (about 4,100 ha on the NAEI figure; our arithmetic).
- On greenhouse gases per hectare alone, the order is clear: Fen water-table raising, then conifer woodland, then broadleaf woodland, then farm trees. The evidence is strongest for the size of the peat saving. It is weakest for farm trees and for livestock in this arable-dominated area.

## Results

**1. Fenland peat water table (DT8; three gases; AR6 GWP100: CH4 27, N2O 273)**
- **Baseline:** drained winter wheat at the Fenland climatological water table (0.66 m), with 200 kg N/ha. Net 37.3 t CO2e/ha/yr: CO2 33.1, N2O 4.3, CH4 ≈ 0 [R1].
- **Reed paludiculture scenarios, net t CO2e/ha/yr [R2]:**
  - 0.0 m: 4.5 (CH4 5.1)
  - 0.1 m: 1.7
  - 0.2 m: 0.9 (the model's optimum)
  - 0.3 m: 3.5
  - 0.5 m: 16.4
- **Avoided emissions against the wheat baseline (our arithmetic):**
  - 0.2 m: ≈36.5 t CO2e/ha/yr, ≈911 t/ha over 25 years
  - 0.1 m: ≈35.6 t CO2e/ha/yr, ≈891 t/ha
  - 0.3 m: ≈33.8 t CO2e/ha/yr, ≈845 t/ha
- **Keep wheat, raise the water table to about 0.4 m:** 21.6 t CO2e/ha/yr, saving ≈15.7 t/ha/yr (≈393 t/ha over 25 years) [R12].
- **Methane trade-off:** raising the water table above about 0.1 m increases CH4 faster than it reduces CO2 (Figure 2).
- **Checks against the UK Peatland Inventory [R9]:**
  - Cropland CO2: model 27.7 vs Tier 2 factor 27.1 t CO2/ha/yr.
  - Rewetted-fen CH4: model 109 vs Tier 2 111 kg CH4/ha/yr.
  - CH4 against the inventory's water-table equation: r = 0.98, NSE 0.88.
  - At intermediate water tables the model's CO2 is lower than the inventory equation: at 0.2 m, −1.1 vs +3.5 t CO2/ha/yr; at 0.3 m, 1.2 vs 8.4.
  - Using the inventory equation at 0.2 m cuts the saving to ≈31.9 t CO2e/ha/yr. Using a reed crop at the drained water table as the baseline (25.8 t [R2]) cuts it to ≈25 t/ha/yr. So a defensible range is about 25–36 t CO2e/ha/yr, or about 620–910 t/ha over 25 years (our arithmetic).
- DT8 gives no ensemble range of its own.

**2. Breckland woodland on arable land (DT4, Thetford pilot; CO2 in biomass, litter and deadwood; no thinning; planted 2026)**
- **Corsican pine,** cumulative net flux to 2050 [R3]:
  - Yield-curve model: −331 t CO2/ha (90 % range −541 to −48)
  - 3-PG model: −342 t CO2/ha (90 % range −553 to −58)
  - Mean −13.2 to −13.7 t CO2/ha/yr, still rising: about −20 to −25 t/ha/yr by year 25.
- **Mixed broadleaves** [R4]:
  - Yield-curve model: −80 t CO2/ha (−127 to −61)
  - 3-PG model: −180 t CO2/ha (−393 to −9)
- **Hold-out validation against Biomass CCI, 2018–2024 [R10]:**
  - Corsican pine: R² 0.63–0.66, RMSE 36–37 Mg AGB/ha, bias +2 Mg/ha.
  - Mixed broadleaves: R² 0.75–0.77.

**3. Fenland hedgerow and agroforestry trees (DT6, Fens pilot tile; planting from 2025; 25 years)**
- **Hedgerow, per 1,000 trees planted [R5]:** −33 t CO2 (size-age model; −64 to −14) or −140 t (crown light-use model; −185 to −99).
- **Agroforestry, per 1,000 trees planted [R6]:** −26 t (−50 to −11) or −109 t (−144 to −77).
- About 77–81 % of trees are alive after 25 years.
- **Rescaled per hectare (our arithmetic, at assumed densities):**
  - Agroforestry at 100 trees/ha: 2.6–10.9 t CO2/ha over 25 years.
  - Hedgerow trees at 10 per ha of farm: 0.33–1.4 t CO2/ha.
- **Validation in the Fens [R11]:** neither model beats a no-growth forecast (skill −0.0002 and −0.35). Both overpredict height growth (0.76 and 2.03 m/yr predicted against 0.33 m/yr observed). The lower, size-age figures are the more credible.

**4. Livestock methane, Fens–Breckland box (DT2)**
- **Totals for 479,557 ha of land [R7]:**
  - 3,055 t CH4/yr: dairy 1,364, beef and other cattle 1,200, sheep 486, goats 5.
  - 82,479 t CO2e/yr (AR6), or 85,534 t (AR5).
  - 32,140 livestock units, stocking 0.23 LSU per hectare of grazing land, 21.9 kg CH4 per hectare of grazing land.
- **Over 25 years at constant herds:** ≈2.06 Mt CO2e (AR6; our arithmetic).
- **Against the NAEI map [R8]:** NAEI gives 5,569 t CH4 (DT2/NAEI ratio 0.55).
  - Spatial correlation is weak here: r = 0.29 at 1 km and 0.44 at 20 km. Nationally it is 0.65 and 0.97.
  - NAEI also includes pigs, poultry, horses and deer, which DT2 does not model.

## What this means

**Peat rewetting dominates per hectare.** Drained Fen peat is one of the most emission-intensive land uses in England in these models. Arable at 37 t CO2e/ha/yr emits more each year, per hectare, than a young conifer stand removes even at its peak rate.

Raising the mean water table to about 0.1–0.3 m avoids most of that emission. Even the conservative version of the calculation (inventory CO2 equation, or a less emissive baseline) gives 25–32 t CO2e/ha/yr. That is about twice the 25-year average removal of Corsican pine and four or more times that of broadleaves.

Unlike woodland, the saving starts in the first years after rewetting rather than building up over decades. It is avoided emission, not removal, so it lasts only while the water table is held up. If the land is drained again, the emissions return.

**Methane limits how high the water table should go.** Above about 0.1 m, methane from the wet peat rises faster than CO2 falls (Figure 2). A target band of roughly 0.1–0.3 m gives the best net result in the model, better than full flooding.

**There is an intermediate option.** Keeping wheat but holding the water table at about 0.4 m still saves about 16 t CO2e/ha/yr. That is more than conifer woodland on Breckland arable, and food production continues. The model does not assess yield effects.

**Woodland gives a solid but slower return.** Corsican pine in Breckland reaches 13–14 t CO2/ha/yr averaged over 25 years, and is still accelerating at year 25. Over 50 years or more the gap with peat would narrow. The 90 % range is wide (about 50–550 t/ha), mainly because productivity varies between sites. Broadleaves on Breckland's poor sandy soils are calibrated at low productivity (yield class 2), which explains their low figure.

**Farm trees are small per hectare.** At realistic densities their climate contribution per hectare is small, and the Fens growth models are not yet validated. Their case rests on other grounds (shelter, soil, landscape, biodiversity), which these twins do not assess.

**Livestock methane is a modest share in this area.** East Anglia is arable-dominated: 0.23 LSU per hectare of grazing land and about 0.17 t CO2e per hectare of all land per year. The area's whole livestock methane (82,000–150,000 t CO2e/yr on the DT2 and NAEI figures) is matched by avoided emissions from rewetting about 2,300–4,100 ha of Fen arable. Livestock policy levers matter more in western grassland regions than here.

**Accounting.** Livestock methane sits in the inventory's Agriculture sector. Peat and tree carbon sit in LULUCF. The rewetting N2O saving (fertiliser and peat-N) is reported in Agriculture.

## Policy relevance

**Bearing on policy**
- **Land Use Framework spatial priorities:** on greenhouse gases per hectare, the results support treating deep drained Fen arable peat as a top-tier candidate for land-use change.
- **Seventh Carbon Budget:** the CCC's pathway relies on peat restoration and woodland creation, and both options are material contributors here.
- **16.5 % canopy target:** woodland and farm trees contribute to it. Rewetting does not.
- **Schemes:** relevant routes are Landscape Recovery and Countryside Stewardship Higher Tier for rewetting and paludiculture, SFI for agroforestry and hedgerow trees, and woodland creation grants together with the Woodland Carbon Code and Peatland Code.

**Options and trade-offs**
1. **Full rewetting to about 0.1–0.3 m with paludiculture.** Largest saving, available quickly. Ends conventional cropping on high-grade land and needs catchment-level water management. Many Fen farms are tenanted, so landlord consent and the allocation of carbon rights would matter.
2. **Partial water-table raising (about 0.4 m) under arable.** About half the saving while cropping continues. Could suit a broader uptake approach.
3. **Breckland woodland.** Removals build up and last longer. Conifers deliver about 2–4 times the broadleaf figure on these soils. The choice involves trade-offs with heathland and other habitat, which these agents do not assess.
4. **Farm trees.** Small greenhouse-gas effect per hectare; their justification lies in co-benefits that are not modelled here.
5. **Livestock measures.** Limited leverage in this area relative to peat.

**Strength of evidence**
- **Peat saving:** calibrated and checked against UK Peatland Inventory Tier 2 factors (good agreement for cropland CO2 and rewetted CH4). The model is more optimistic than the inventory's CO2 equation at intermediate water tables. Rated: calibrated, moderate to high confidence in the size and ranking, lower in the exact optimum.
- **Woodland:** calibrated to satellite biomass, with hold-out R² of 0.63–0.77. Rated: validated for existing stands, indicative for new planting on arable.
- **Farm trees:** rated indicative only, as there is no hold-out skill.
- **Livestock in East Anglia:** rated indicative, since it is about half the NAEI value.

**What would change the conclusion**
- Field or eddy-covariance data from rewetted Fen sites, to confirm CO2 at 0.2–0.4 m water tables.
- Mapping of peat depth and how much is left.
- Soil-carbon change under new Breckland woodland.
- Food-output and leakage analysis.
- A longer (50-year) horizon, which would favour woodland relatively.

## Possible next steps

- Run the comparison at three uptake levels (for example 10, 25 and 50 % of eligible Fen arable peat and Breckland arable) to give landscape totals against the carbon-budget path.
- Re-run DT8 rewetting with zero fertiliser N, and DT4 over 50 years, to test how sensitive the ranking is.
- Assimilate observed CH4 fluxes from rewetted Fen sites into DT8, and use DT2 assimilate_naei to correct the East Anglia livestock allocation.

## Caveats

- The per-hectare figures are not on exactly the same basis. Rewetting counts all three gases (CO2, CH4, N2O). Woodland and farm trees count only CO2 in biomass, litter and deadwood, against bare land. Soil carbon change under trees, emissions from establishing them, wood products and avoided fertiliser N2O are left out.
- DT8 runs one representative climate year with synthetic seasonal drivers. It gives no uncertainty range. It assumes the target water table is achieved and held all year round on deep peat, and it uses an established regime (3 years after rewetting). Shallow or wasted peat and the practicalities of water supply and level management are not modelled.
- Rewetting uses a reed crop for CO2 and CH4 but the winter-wheat fertiliser regime for N2O. Without fertiliser, N2O would probably be lower, which this run did not test. Loss of food output and its displacement elsewhere (leakage) are not quantified.
- Thetford stands in for Breckland and the DT6 Fens 5 km tile stands in for Fenland farms. The planting densities (100 agroforestry trees/ha, 10 hedgerow trees/ha) are our assumptions. The DT6 Fens models have no hold-out skill [R11].
- DT2 livestock in this box is half the NAEI value, and the spatial match is poor (r 0.29 at 1 km). Treat 3,055–5,569 t CH4/yr as the range. Pigs and poultry are not covered.
- Biodiversity, water quality, flood risk, food production and farm income are not modelled by these agents.
- Results are what-if model responses, not observations or verified credits.
- Results from pilot landscapes are indicative for wider areas; scaling them up assumes the pilot is representative, which this report does not test.
- The digital twins estimate biophysical effects; costs, payment rates, uptake behaviour and Green Book appraisal are outside their scope.
- Policy context reflects the position in November 2026; check current Defra guidance before relying on it.
