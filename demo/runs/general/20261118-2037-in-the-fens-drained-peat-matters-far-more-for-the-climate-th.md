# In the Fens, drained peat matters far more for the climate than livestock or farm trees

*Prepared for: A Fenland resident (general public) · 18 November 2026 · SLATE digital-twin orchestrator*

**Question.** I live in the Fens. Which matters more for the climate here: the drained peat fields, the cattle and sheep, or the trees in hedges and around farms? What could change it?

**As understood.** Where you live in the Fens, which has the biggest climate effect: drained peat farmland, cattle and sheep, or trees in hedges and around farms? And what could change that? We used the SLATE Cambridgeshire Fens pilot area, a 5 km × 5 km square (OS grid tile TL4075, along the Great Ouse washes near Sutton and Earith). Peat results use the East Anglian Fens calibration of the peat model.

## Key findings

- Each hectare of drained peat farmland gives off about 23–37 t CO2e a year, depending on the crop (sugar beet 23.2, potato 37.0, winter wheat 37.3; AR6 GWP100). Most of this is CO2 from the peat slowly breaking down [R2–R4].
- All the cattle and sheep in the 25 km² pilot square together produce about 24 t of methane a year, or 640 t CO2e (AR6) [R6]. Simple arithmetic: just 17–28 ha of drained peat cropland gives off as much as all those animals.
- The trees outside woodland in the square (about 2,400 trees, mostly willows, poplars and trees around farmsteads) hold about 1,290 t CO2 in living wood. They are modelled to take up roughly 110–120 t CO2 a year [R8, R9]. That offsets about 3–5 ha of drained peat.
- Raising the water table is by far the biggest lever. In the model, keeping water about 0.2 m below the surface (for example with wet farming such as reed) cuts the net balance to about 2.0 t CO2e/ha/yr, saving roughly 35 t CO2e per hectare each year [R5, R2].
- Livestock options have much smaller effects at this scale. A 20% cut in cattle enteric methane (from a feed additive) saves about 97 t CO2e/yr across the whole square, and 25% fewer animals saves about 160 t CO2e/yr (AR6) [R12, R13]. Planting 1,000 new hedgerow trees takes up about 53 t CO2 over 30 years in total (range 22–104 t) [R14].

## Results

**Drained peat (DT8).** These figures assume the typical drained Fenland water table, about 0.66 m below the surface. The modelled net greenhouse-gas balance per hectare per year is:
- winter wheat: 37.3 t CO2e (CO2 33.1, N2O 4.3, CH4 ≈ 0) [R2]
- potato: 37.0 t CO2e (CO2 29.8, N2O 7.2) [R3]
- sugar beet: 23.2 t CO2e (CO2 18.4, N2O 4.9) [R4]

The model agrees well with the UK Peatland Inventory. For cropland CO2 it gives 27.7 t CO2/ha against the inventory's 27.1. For N2O it gives 16.3 kg N/ha, matching the inventory. Its methane predictions correlate with the inventory relationship at r = 0.98 [R11].

**Cattle and sheep (DT2).** The 25 km² square holds about 98 dairy cattle, 170 beef and other cattle, 436 sheep and a few goats, based on the 2020 June Survey allocated to the map. Together they emit 23.7 t CH4 a year: 20.2 t from digestion (enteric) and 3.5 t from manure. That is 640 t CO2e (AR6), or 664 t CO2e using AR5 [R6]. Dairy and beef cattle make up over 90% of this. Within 10 km the total is 284 t CH4, or 7,660 t CO2e (AR6) [R7]. Spread over the square's land, livestock come to about 0.26 t CO2e per hectare a year (our arithmetic: 640 t ÷ 2,492 ha).

**Trees in hedges and around farms (DT6).** The square has 856 tree objects, about 2,379 trees and 13.4 ha of canopy. Together they hold 353 t C, or 1,293 t CO2, in living biomass, measured from lidar in 2021. The 90% range for above-ground biomass is 364–756 t [R8]. Over 2026–2055 the model estimates net uptake of about 102–120 t CO2 a year, an average of 110 t, or 3,290 t over 30 years [R9]. However, the living tree carbon itself falls slightly, from 344 to 302 t C, because older trees die. The uptake figure counts carbon that passes into soil and deadwood.

**Bringing them together (Figure 1).** The square has 1,590 ha of arable land [R10], but the agents do not map how much of it sits on peat. If all of it were drained peat, it would emit roughly 37,000–59,000 t CO2e a year, about 58–93 times the livestock total. Even if only a tenth were peat, it would still emit several times more than the livestock (our arithmetic on [R2–R4, R10]).

**What could change it.**
- *Water table (Figure 2, [R5]).* The modelled net balance is 37.1 t CO2e/ha/yr at 0.9 m depth, 19.4 at 0.5 m, 5.5 at 0.3 m, 2.0 at 0.2 m (the optimum), 2.1 at 0.1 m and 4.7 at the surface. The rise at the very wettest settings comes from methane.
- *Livestock (whole square).* A feed additive cutting cattle enteric methane by 20% gives −15% (640 → 543 t CO2e AR6) [R12]. Cutting all herds by 25% gives −25% (640 → 480 t CO2e) [R13].
- *Trees.* 1,000 new hedgerow trees, with 85% surviving establishment, would hold 3.5 t C after 30 years and take up 4.5 t CO2 a year by then [R14].

## What this means

For the Fens, the answer is clear: **the drained peat matters most, by a very wide margin.** Peat is a huge store of carbon built up over thousands of years. Draining it for farming lets air in, and the peat slowly breaks down and releases CO2. Fertiliser on peat soils adds nitrous oxide. One hectare of drained peat under wheat or potatoes gives off about 37 t CO2e a year. That is more than the livestock emit per hectare across the whole square, more than a hundred times over.

Livestock are a real but much smaller source here, because the Fens are mostly arable with few grazing animals. Stocking is about 0.29 livestock units per hectare of grazing land [R6]. Hedge and farmstead trees are a small sink. They are worth keeping, but their yearly uptake in the whole square is about the same as the emissions from 3–5 ha of drained peat.

What would change the picture is the water table. The model shows emissions falling steeply as water is brought closer to the surface. Most of the benefit comes from getting the water table into the top 20–30 cm. Even raising it part of the way, to 0.5 m, roughly halves emissions per hectare. Flooding completely is not better than keeping it about 0.2 m down, because waterlogged soils start making methane. By comparison, livestock and tree options have small effects at this scale. One hectare kept wet at about 0.2 m saves roughly as much each year as a feed additive given to every cow in the square, about a third as much in fact (our arithmetic: 35 vs 97 t CO2e/yr).

Rewetting has costs and trade-offs that the models do not cover. These include food production and farm incomes, flood management and drainage boards, and changes for wildlife. Raising the water table on one field also depends on the land around it.

## Caveats

- The agents do not map peat depth or where peat lies. Much Fenland arable is on silt or thin, wasted peat, which emits less. So peat totals for an area are illustrative, and only the per-hectare figures are model estimates.
- The peat model uses one representative year of synthetic Fenland climate, not measurements from a specific field. Water-table results are modelled responses, not observations.
- A separate grass-on-peat run at 0.3 m showed a net sink of 4.3 t CO2e/ha/yr [R15]. This conflicts with the reed water-table scenario and probably leaves out carbon removed in harvested grass, so we have not relied on it.
- Livestock numbers come from the 2020 June Survey, allocated to a 1 km grid using national emission factors. Individual farms may differ. Methane CO2e uses AR6 (27). The AR5 figures are about 4% higher.
- Tree uptake depends on the growth model. In the Fens pilot it showed no forecasting skill over a simple no-growth assumption on a short 1.4-year hold-out test [R1]. Hedges under 3 m tall and woodland are not included.
- The pilot square stands in for 'the Fens'. Your own neighbourhood may have more or less peat, livestock and trees.
- Peat CO2 is reported in a different part of the national inventory (land use) from livestock methane (agriculture). Both are treated here as yearly climate effects on the same AR6 GWP100 basis.
